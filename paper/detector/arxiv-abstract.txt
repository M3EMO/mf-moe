Mixture-of-experts (MoE) language models compute a routing distribution over experts at every layer and token. We ask how much these router signals reveal about whether a generated token belongs to a wrong answer, how they compare with output entropy and a hidden-state probe, and what they cost to read in serving. A linear detector on router and output statistics, calibrated with Platt scaling and thresholded by conformal risk control, has a higher token-level AUROC than output entropy in every factual setting we measured, across Granite 3.1 1B-A400M, gpt-oss-20b and OLMoE-1B-7B. Paired bootstraps show the difference is significant on Granite, on gpt-oss in vLLM and on four of five OLMoE test sets, but not on NQ-Open. The size of the gain depends on how it is measured: the 0.906 AUROC on Granite comes from a test set where 92% of tokens are errors; on 2,600 gpt-oss questions, pooling non-arithmetic types gives 0.723 against 0.556 for output entropy. Per answer, on 1,792 non-arithmetic factual answers, adding router statistics to question type and length raises cross-validated AUROC from 0.679 to 0.811 (point estimates), while output entropy adds at most 0.03. Router signals stay within 0.02 AUROC of a hidden-state probe, fall short of the published InnerExpert detector on its own protocol (0.651 vs. 0.762), and on code mostly act as a tie-breaker. Their advantage is cost: 4.7% added latency in HuggingFace on an L4 GPU, and no detectable cost in vLLM.

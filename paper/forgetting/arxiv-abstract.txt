When a language model is fine-tuned to change one fact, what does it forget? We study this with preregistered experiments on Granite 3.1 1B-A400M, a mixture-of-experts model, and repeat the certainty and source experiments on the dense OLMo-2-1B. For facts learned by fine-tuning, forgetting is directional: the reverse direction loses about a tenth of the forward damage. For facts known from pretraining, the damage is partially shared after a short overwrite (ratio 0.33) and shared after a long one (0.61). In a preregistered test, Granite is certainty-blind: the prior confidence of a fact does not predict how much an overwrite changes it (Spearman +0.005 [-0.096, +0.103]). The information needed to change this is inside the model: a linear probe on the hidden state tracks certainty (rho = 0.62), and weighting each training example by the probe yields 83% of the selectivity of an oracle that knows the true loss. The probe gate replicated on OLMo-2 but failed its preregistered rules on a second Granite fact bank. A threshold that opens only after enough distinct phrasings rejects a single repeated voice and accepts ten, and an unlabeled similarity counter separates distinct sources from paraphrases. Damage to untouched facts is a separate axis: EWC cuts it by 61% and yields half of the oracle's selectivity. A sparse expansion modeled on the fruit-fly mushroom body multiplies collateral damage by 3.7. We report every preregistered rule, including the failures.
